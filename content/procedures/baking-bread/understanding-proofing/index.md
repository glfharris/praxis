## Short answer

Look for dough that has expanded and feels aerated. During the final prove, a gentle indentation should return slowly, leaving a slight mark. Use several clues together: no single test gives a perfect answer.

This is a published, non-clinical demonstration of the supporting-explainer format.

## Two different rises

### First rise

The whole batch rises before shaping. For the example white dough, look for approximately doubled volume and a softer, airier feel. A straight-sided container makes the change easier to see.

### Final prove

The shaped loaf or rolls rise before baking. They should become visibly puffy while retaining their shape. Handle them gently at this stage.

## Reading the signs

| Observation | Possible interpretation |
| --- | --- |
| Firm dough that springs back immediately | It may need longer. |
| Puffy dough with a slowly returning indentation | It may be ready to bake. |
| Fragile dough that collapses when touched | It may have proved too far. |

{% <callout kind="note" title="A guide, not a precise test"> %}
Wet dough, cold dough and different flours behave differently. Compare the feel with how the dough looked at the start, as well as its response to a finger press.
{% </callout> %}

## Why timing changes

A warm kitchen usually speeds fermentation; a cool one slows it down. Water temperature, yeast quantity and the dough recipe also affect the pace. Treat the times in the guides as checkpoints.

{% <details title="Keep a simple baking note"> %}
Record the room temperature, approximate rising times and the finished result. A few notes from your own kitchen can be more useful than repeatedly aiming for an exact time from a recipe.
{% </details> %}
