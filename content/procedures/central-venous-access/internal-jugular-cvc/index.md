## Indications

Insertion of central venous access is not without risk, therefore there should always be a conscious decision prior to placement. The common reasons for central venous access are:

- Vasotoxic medications
- Parenteral nutrition
- Difficult peripheral access Chemotherapy — PICC often preferred Advanced haemodynamic and CVP monitoring
## Contraindications

There are few absolute contraindications for central venous access, aside from refusal by a capacitous patient, and the decision to place a central venous catheter should be taken with heed to the clinical context. Relative contraindications include:

- Severely abnormal clotting — see [coagulation thresholds](@/procedures/central-venous-access/coagulation-thresholds/index.md)
- Overlying infection
- Ipsilateral thrombus
- Uncooperative patient

## Equipment

{% <checklist title="Equipment"> %}
- CVC kit with introducer needle, guidewire, dilator, scalpel and suture
- Ultrasound machine with high-frequency linear probe, sterile probe cover and sterile gel
- Full aseptic barrier: hat, mask, sterile gown, gloves, large drape
- Skin prep — 2% chlorhexidine with 70% alcohol
- 1% lidocaine in a 10 mL syringe with a small hypodermic needle
- Saline flushes for each lumen with sterile injection ports
- An assistant
{% </checklist> %}

## Preparation

Consent the patient if they are awake, warning them about expected sensations, while reassuring them that you will be using local anaesthetic.

Scanning the patient's neck prior to establishing a sterile field can be useful to identify possible anatomical complexity, measure approximate depth to vessel, and evaluate the relation of the internal jugular to the carotid.

Place an inco pad under the patients neck and shoulder to protect their bedding. Consider placing a hudson mask over the patient's face, which can support the drapes away from the patient's face.

Ensure appropropriate monitoring -- typically ECG, blood pressure, and pulse oximetry.

Identify a willing assistant who can assist with positioning, reassuring the patient, and fetching equipment after the operator has scrubbed.

## Positioning

Place the patient in a head-down tilt, also known as the Trendelenburg position, to increase the filling and visibility of the vessel as well as reducing the risk of air embolism.

Turn their head slightly away from the side of insertion, opening up the available space. Too much rotation can lead to an overlap between the jugular and carotid, increasing the risk of accidental arterial puncture.

## Procedure

{% <step number={1} title="Prepare equipment"> %}
Open your equipment in a sterile fashion. Flush each lumen of the catheter and then close off the line so the flush is retained within it. Finally, arrange your equipment so that each item is easy to reach in the order you'll need it.
{% </step> %}

{% <step number={2} title="Prepare sterile field"> %}
If a beard will interfere with the procedure or dressing, consider shaving or trimming the area first.

Remove overlying dressings, tube ties, ECG dots from area.

Clean the skin with 2% chlorhexidine, and apply sterile drapes.
Most drapes have an orientation and a transparent window, which can reduce the feeling of claustrophobia for the patient.
{% </step> %}

{% <step number={3} title="Scan and identify vessels"> %}
Scan the neck to identify the vessels, paying particular attention to distinguishing the internal jugular vein from the carotid artery — use Doppler if there is any uncertainty. Also look for overlying superficial vessels, such as the external jugular vein, before choosing your approach.

{% <callout kind="tip"> %}
The artery is typically more medial, rounder and thicker walled. It will pulsate on pressure from your probe and be more difficult to compress.
{% </callout> %}
{% </step> %}

{% <step number={4} title="Inject local anaesthetic"> %}
Once you have selected your site, infiltrate with small volumes of 1% lidocaine subcutaneously (this step may not be necessary if the patient is heavily sedated). Keep the volume modest, as too much can distort the local anatomy and make the vessels harder to identify.
{% </step> %}

{% <step number={5} title="Access vessel"> %}
Attach the syringe to the needle or cannula and insert through the skin. Holding the probe with your non-dominant hand, insert the needle under direct visualisation towards the vein. Aspirate as you're advancing through the wall to know when you're within the lumen itself as opposed to the wall.

{% <callout kind="tip"> %}
A cannula can be more fiddly to use than a needle, but may help reduce the risk of passing through the back wall of the vein or of the needle tip slipping out while you're inserting the guidewire.
{% </callout> %}
{% </step> %}

{% <step number={6} title="Insert guidewire"> %}
Insert the guidewire through the needle or cannula, keeping hold of it at all times.

There are markings on the guidewire to know how far you have inserted it. A distance of 15–18 cm is standard.

Ensure you are not forcing the wire at any point, as this may indicate that it has kinked or is abutting a vessel wall.

Have your assistant keep an eye on the cardiac monitor during guidewire insertion to assess for ectopic beats, which may indicate the wire has advanced too far into the right heart.
{% </step> %}

{% <step number={7} title="Confirm guidewire position"> %}
Use ultrasound to confirm the wire lies within the IJV and is moving freely, scanning as far caudally as possible to check it stays within the lumen along its course. Both in-plane and out-of-plane views are worth using. Remove the needle or cannula, leaving the guidewire in place.
{% </step> %}

{% <step number={8} title="Dilate skin and vessel"> %}
Decide whether to make a small skin incision or to dilate without cutting.

{% <callout kind="tip"> %}
If you're dilating without an incision, wet the dilator with saline first so it passes smoothly through the skin.
{% </callout> %}

Insert the dilator at the same angle as your original needle insertion. Throughout dilation, check that the guidewire continues to move smoothly; resistance may mean it is kinking or that a false passage is being created. When withdrawing the dilator, press gauze over the insertion site to hold the guidewire in place.

Some bleeding is common after dilation, so it's worth leaving gauze over the site to control this.
{% </step> %}

{% <step number={9} title="Insert catheter and remove guidewire"> %}
Hold the guidewire with one hand while threading the catheter over it with the other, feeding it into the insertion site — expect some bleeding at this stage.

{% <callout kind="tip"> %}
Forming a loop in the guidewire and holding it near the end reduces movement of the tip, which makes threading easier. Remember that the wire will emerge from the distal port, so make sure this is unblocked beforehand to let the wire feed through.
{% </callout> %}
{% </step> %}

{% <step number={10} title="Secure catheter"> %}
Attach the catheter clamp and place four sutures to secure the catheter to the skin.
{% </step> %}

{% <step number={11} title="Aspirate and flush lumens"> %}
Fit bungs or injection ports onto each lumen to protect the line, then aspirate and flush each one. Use a 10 mL syringe for flushing, as smaller syringes generate higher pressures that risk damaging the vessel wall.
{% </step> %}

{% <step number={12} title="Apply dressing"> %}
Apply a dressing over the site, choosing between a chlorhexidine-impregnated dressing or a standard dressing according to local policy.
{% </step> %}

## Checks

Confirm correct placement and rule out immediate complications before the line is used. Some checks are made during the procedure itself, while others are performed once the catheter is in place:

- Ultrasound confirmation at multiple steps
- Ensure the guidewire moves freely during dilation
- Consider VBG
- CXR — exclude pneumothorax and malposition; see [chest radiograph confirmation](@/procedures/central-venous-access/chest-radiograph-confirmation/index.md)
- Transduce — confirm CVP waveform

## Complications

- Retained guidewire
- Pneumothorax
- Failure
- Bleeding
- Blood-stream infection
- Catheter-associated thrombus
- Arterial dilatation — stroke
- Air embolus
- Arrhythmia
- Vascular or cardiac injury from guidewire
- Consider superficial cervical plexus block if awake
